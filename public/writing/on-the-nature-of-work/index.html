On the Nature of Work

We are in a company. There is the sanitation team, the tech team, and every other team which usually make up a company. We’ll use the Euros as our currency.

The tech team has entry-level positions paid 50 thousand per year, while the most experienced positions are paid 250 thousand per year. A ratio of 1:5.

There are no corporate hierarchies here. No bosses. Some key leadership duties are rotated amongst the more experienced staff, but everyone (bar first year recruits) has a direct say and vote in most decision making. More consequential decisions may use the pay ratio to determine the worth of each vote. Direct democracy in the workplace.

The experienced workers guide the entry-level workers on the know-how.

The entry-level learn and bring their new ideas.

Rotational job roles are encouraged (but not enforced).

Most promotions are made based on time spent (with room for rapid promotions for the more exceptional ones), so that in a few years, you may expect the “entry-level” to own the responsibilities and pay of the “most experienced” ones.

I see some similarities with our education system here, do you?

If the company grows, and salaries increase, it increases together; based on that ratio. For example: 60 thousand : 300 thousand per year.

Now, the sanitation team also follow the same framework, and pay.

Our entry-level tech team earns 50 thousand, our entry-level sanitation team earns 50 thousand.

Our experienced tech team earns 250 thousand, our experienced sanitation team earns 250 thousand.

What resistance did you conjure to this model? I’ll try to address some of them.

If one feels the sanitation job is too “easy” or “unskilled,” they can switch to that team. Or, better still, they shouldn’t hire any sanitation team since it’s that easy; a cleaning rota can help – like in schools – it’ll help the workforce be more sustainable and cleanliness conscious, while saving the company’s pocket. Just look at how cleanliness is viewed in space stations.

If they say, I spent “so so” years learning this tech skill, so “such and such”. It’s important for them to note that a few of the most successful tech folks don’t spend much time in universities. They only spend a couple, or less, years. Still, people in the broader tech ecosystem – whose learning time could take less than two years – initially earn substantially more than doctors and other traditional five years courses, without the debt baggage.

If sanitation staff are paid equally and fairly, there’ll be less stigma, and a more conscious effort to make the job more professional. There should be schools or skill centres on sanitation work – check out what Germany is doing – with some education on the biochemistry of germs and material sciences. We could also imagine a kind of bio-safety technician with AI robotics specialisations.

You could posit: “there would be over-saturation of workers in the experienced category, and the workplace culture will breed laziness.” To which I’ll rebut below:

There’ll be a healthy periodic review of the employment status of staff who don’t meet a transparently set performance criteria. Whether in the entry-level category, some middle-level category, or the experienced category. This should discourage laziness, disillusion, or “kicking back”. It will also help lower the staff numbers at any given moment, and create openings for fresh recruits with fresh ideas.

Despite the above, the preference would always be to retain all existing staff – no matter their category or pay – moving towards a future where there’s indeed an over-saturation of workers in the experienced category, and thus, egalitarianism.

Talent retention is a different beast; and this is why this model may work best if codified into law – a non-capitalist society. But since this may not happen anytime soon, our company may not be able to compete for the most prodigious talent. Here are three things we can do.

Firstly, the culture needed in the company is not one of righteous capitalist greed, but of contentment and drive to do things to help the world. And as such, if one leaves because of better pay – it may mean they weren’t the right fit for our culture. It’s not like anyone will be paid less than a comfortable wage. A selling point would be our culture – to the right audience, that is.

Secondly, most of our staff would be in the more technical arm of the company. If the sanitation job is not as important to the company’s growth, then we’ll only need a few sanitation staff who’ll cover as much to justify their pay. Or, the technical staff rotate the sanitation tasks. This will help free more funds for salaries.

Thirdly, we should remember that the salaries increase as the company grows – in a ratio within a department, and equally with other departments. So it’s not a stagnant environment, just a more dignifying one.

It’s important to note that similar frameworks to all these are out there – the Mondragon co-operatives, the Israeli Kibbutz before the 2000s, the US Navy (a character in Kim Stanley Robinson’s The Ministry for the Future, put theirs at 1:8), and the United Nations. They all use their chosen ratios / actually pay equally – 1:5 was arbitrarily chosen by me for this piece.

But while researching for this piece, I found the most striking example – even more egalitarian than my own framework. Suma Wholefoods, a workers co-operative which has been active since 1977, shows that equal pay is very much sustainable and breeds a lauded culture which still attracts high talent (with a less capitalist mindset) despite the uncompetitive pay.

No matter how egalitarian a company culture is, if the workers have vastly unequal wages, a silent and ingrained hierarchy will build up. A class system, scrunching up of the noses at certain jobs, and everyone will take the job – no matter how important it is for the society – as a “third-class job”.

All jobs are equal. If a job is too “basic” for almost-equal pay, that job should be scrapped and done by the general workforce in the company.

To add: everyone, irrespective of their employment status, deserves a liveable income (with dignity). This includes unemployed. Their basic needs should be provided by the society, though not up to the “50 thousand” entry-level salary. A 1:3 ratio with the entry-level salary wouldn’t be bad (17 thousand : 50 thousand). This would work better if our model is codified into law, and as such, is just a solidarity addition to this piece.

Anthropic recently released an article on employment in the age of AI. While it made no mention of the egalitarian leaning of this piece, it’s worth a look to understand how and why we need to change the nature of work.

Note: I used “sanitation” work to make my case, but any societally agreed “lowly” work will do.

Also, if you still disagree on the importance of certain jobs, see what happened in 1968, New York, when some folks disagreed on the importance of a certain job. Hint: the city had some “sanitary” issues, and came to a standstill.
